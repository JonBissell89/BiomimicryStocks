What this is

An engine that measures where civilization is furthest from balance, which way each system must move to correct, and which public companies accelerate that correction.

It is not looking for good companies; there is no such measurement. It starts one level above companies, with the species itself, and only reaches the ranked table at the end of a chain that begins with the planet.

Step 0 · The diagnosis

Imagine a scientist discovering Homo sapiens for the first time

A scientist walks into a rainforest and finds a species never described before. The scientist does not know its currencies, its political parties, its stock markets or its industries, and does not ask about them. They measure what ecology measures for any organism: population, energy use, resource flows, waste streams, habitat modification, cooperation, competition, and the feedback loops created by its tools. Then they ask the question ecology always asks: is this species moving toward a relationship with its environment that can persist?

Seen from outside, Homo sapiens consumes energy and matter, occupies habitat, moves resources, produces waste, modifies its niche, reproduces, cooperates, competes, builds networks, stores information, and grows external organs in the form of tools and technology. Civilization is a metabolism. Money is not the underlying system; it is an information layer the species built to coordinate access to energy, matter, labour, land and time. Technology has let the species push many natural feedbacks further away in time. That does not eliminate the feedback. It accumulates it.

So the first question this engine asks has nothing to do with companies: where is the metabolism of Homo sapiens furthest from a stable regenerative state?

Balance is not a fixed point, because nothing in nature holds still. Wolves multiply until they outrun the deer; the crash feeds the deer's recovery, and the deer's recovery feeds the wolves'. Tree stocks grow, burn, die and regrow. Every living system breathes like this, and the ebb and flow is not imbalance; it is what balance actually looks like. So this map never scores movement as getting better or worse. A safe range here means the envelope a stock's own oscillations have stayed inside for as long as the record runs. A stock inside its envelope is in rhythm, however fast it is moving. Imbalance is narrower and checkable: the stock is outside its envelope, or moving at a rate the record does not show. Atmospheric CO2 held between 180 and 300 parts per million through every glacial cycle of the last 800,000 years; it now reads near 425, moving on a clock about a hundred times faster than the glacial swings.

Rate is a measurement here, not a verdict, and it cuts both ways: a swing runs at the speed of whatever drives it, in both directions. Wolves swing at wolf speed out and back. This excursion is driven by the species' own capability, so the return leg can run on the same fast clock the outbound leg did; ozone left its envelope in about fifty years and is returning on the same order, which no natural ozone process ever did. The flow turns at the driver's speed, and how fast the stock itself comes home also depends on its own drain rate, which is what the irreversibility term prices. The correction arrives either way, the way it arrives for the wolves, and which arm it takes is decided by access to the alternative: the wolves have no second prey, so their correction is a crash, while the makers of CFCs had substitutes at hand, so the ozone correction was a treaty.

A stock can leave its envelope because the species takes too much of something, produces too much of something, regenerates too little of it, or because an essential need stays undersupplied while plenty is consumed getting it wrong. That gives the only two labels the map uses, and every row carries one:

↗Ecological overshoot. Human throughput exceeds the regenerative or assimilative capacity of the surrounding system.
↙Provisioning deficit. The system fails to provide an essential human need adequately despite consuming substantial resources.

The future lies where both shrink at once: more human need satisfied with less energy, material extraction, waste, scarcity and ecological disruption. That is the bridge between biomimicry and a resource-based economy, and nothing on this page treats it as guaranteed.

Wherever science provides a quantitative control variable, the map uses it instead of opinion. Seven of nine planetary-boundary processes currently sit outside their safe operating space. Extinctions run above 100 per million species-years against a boundary below 10. Human appropriation of net primary production is roughly 30 percent against a boundary near 10. Those are exactly the numbers an ecologist observing Homo sapiens from outside the species would write down.

Where civilization is furthest from balance

Each row is a civilization-scale stock. Severity is its distance from equilibrium: distance from the safe range x load-bearing importance x rate of divergence x scale of exposure x irreversibility, on an open instrument whose ceiling of 486 is deliberately lethal. That reading would mean a load-bearing global stock past a hard limit for the species, effectively permanent, and still diverging fast; nothing on the map is anywhere near it, and the top of the scale is reserved for readings that would actually mean the species cannot live there. Beside severity, each row carries its spring: turn pressure, distance x pressure x access x coupling out of 108, reading how hard the counterforce is pushing back and how reachable the alternative is. A far-out stock with a loaded spring is a predictable flip headed for a managed return; a far-out stock with a slack spring is the dangerous kind, and when access is the missing factor, what eventually turns it is the die-off arm. As AI and automation raise access across many systems at once, these spring readings should rise together; that is the convergence hypothesis measured in place. Open any row for the full chain: the stock's own record over time, its state, movement relative to the safe range, the natural rhythm where a record exists, the physical flows causing the excursion, the counterforce gathering against them and the alternative's reachability, the turn pressure they produce, the required correction, the clock that correction runs on, and the public companies in this universe attached to it. The imbalance is established first and exists whether or not any company serves it; a correction with no company yet is listed as exactly that.

Severity reads distance from equilibrium, not moral urgency and not a prediction. None of it is a danger to the planet itself: planets have no preferred state, and Earth has run far hotter and far colder than anything here. The envelope belongs to the species, the range every system Homo sapiens depends on was built inside. Stratospheric ozone stays on the map at zero as the one completed return: a stock that left its envelope and was brought back inside it. Each chart is sampled weekly; most of the underlying science re-measures yearly or slower, so a flat stretch means the record has not updated, not that the stock stood still. The labels are not opinions about the charts: movement and, where a control variable exists, distance are derived from each stock's record by the engine and audited on every build, so a written label that disagrees with a measurement does not survive. Definitions, anchors and sources live in the engine repository, and the map is re-researched, not assumed.

The whole engine runs on one ordering, and the ordering is the discipline: nothing to the right is examined until everything to its left is established. This is what keeps it from discovering an attractive company first and rationalizing why it matters afterward. The imbalance must exist independently of the company.

The convergence hypothesis, stated so it can fail

Underneath the map sits one falsifiable question, and this site is an instrument for testing it: as technological capability increases, does civilization systematically move toward greater abundance of essential outcomes while requiring less scarce material, energy and human labour per outcome?

The mechanisms that would drive it are each measurable on their own curve. AI lowers the cost of intelligence and coordination. Automation lowers the labour required per unit of production. Renewable and advanced energy systems lower the resource cost of usable energy. Precision agriculture and biological systems lower inputs per unit of nutrition. Closed-loop manufacturing lowers virgin material demand. Distributed production lowers transportation and coordination requirements. Preventive and regenerative medicine lowers the resources required to maintain health. If these converge, economic organization can shift from maximizing resource throughput toward maximizing useful human outcomes per unit of physical resource consumed.

If the hypothesis is right, companies that make essential outcomes cheaper, more abundant, regenerative, distributed and resource-efficient should progressively capture larger physical and economic flows, and the tracked record on this site should show it. If it is wrong, the same record should eventually demonstrate that too. Either answer is the experiment working.

The governing rule for everything here: do not predict the future by extrapolating markets. Measure the imbalance. Determine the direction the physical system must move to resolve it. Measure the clock. Then find the mechanisms carrying civilization in that direction. The crystal ball is not a prediction. The crystal ball is the distance from equilibrium.

Step 1 · The idea

Balance is a physical property, not a virtue

Nothing in nature is good or bad. Systems are simply in balance or out of it, and everything inside them is constantly pushed back toward equilibrium. Waste gets consumed by something. Concentration gets dispersed. An organism that spends more energy than it captures dies, and a population that outruns its food supply corrects. Nothing is exempt and nothing is forgiven; imbalance is a debt that always comes due, and the only variable is when.

A living system has no opinion about its parts. An organ that moves resources efficiently, wastes little and costs the body less persists; one that consumes more than it returns is carried for a while and then shed. Not because it was wicked, but because the arithmetic of energy and materials caught up with it. Every ecosystem, every watershed, every body runs on that same self-correction.

Economies are not outside this, because they are made of energy, materials, labour and time. When a company defers its real costs onto a water table, a health system, a supply chain, or a customer who cannot afford the alternative, the cost does not vanish. It accumulates somewhere in the system as an imbalance, and imbalances get corrected: by regulation, by substitution, by exhaustion of the thing being drawn down, or by collapse. Reading a business through that lens is not idealism, it is accounting over a longer horizon than a quarter.

So the scorecard is not a morality test and it does not ask what a company deserves. It asks a mechanical question: does this business help its system settle, by closing loops, cutting the energy and cost per useful outcome, spreading capacity instead of concentrating it, and repairing instead of depleting? Or does it work against that settling, and therefore hold a debt the system will eventually collect?

Answering that mechanically means being precise about what is actually out of balance. It is never the company. It is some quantity that accumulates: water in an aquifer, homes in a city, safe units in a blood supply, carbon in a soil, spare capacity on a grid. Those quantities are the things that can be drained or overfilled, and a company is only ever a mechanism attached to the flow running into or out of one of them. Step 2 turns that into the six measures that produce the score.

Some business models are disqualified outright, however profitable they look right now: weapons, addiction, artificial scarcity, planned obsolescence, and revenue that depends on people staying sick or water staying scarce. Not because they are evil, but because each one earns by holding a system out of balance on purpose. That is a position with a bill attached, and the bill is what eventually arrives. The full rules are at the bottom.

The important catch: being aligned with balance tells you which way a system will push over decades. It does not tell you that this particular company will still be standing to capture it, since plenty of well-aligned businesses run out of cash first. That question is kept completely separate from the score. It is checked first, as a pass or fail, and a company that fails it is set aside no matter how well it scores. Two companies on this list did.

Direction is only half of it. The clock is the other half.

Balance says which way a system is pushing. It says nothing about when the push arrives, and that timing is not set by you. It is set by whatever mechanism does the correcting, and those mechanisms run on wildly different clocks.

A system can sit out of balance for what a person experiences as an entire investing life while barely registering on the clock of the thing that will eventually correct it. An aquifer being drawn down faster than it recharges is in obvious imbalance, and it can stay that way for eighty years. A soil losing carbon corrects over centuries. To a forest that is a moment; to a solar system it is nothing at all; to someone holding a stock it is longer than they will hold anything. Being right about the imbalance and wrong about the clock is indistinguishable from being wrong.

Months to a few yearsSubstitution when a cheaper option already exists. Reimbursement and pricing decisions. Anything where a buyer can simply switch. Fast enough to trade.
Years to a decadeRegulation, litigation, standards changes, technology displacing an incumbent. The zone this list mostly lives in.
DecadesInfrastructure replacement cycles, demographic shifts, resource depletion becoming expensive rather than merely visible. Longer than most holding periods.
Centuries and beyondSoil, aquifers, oceans, climate. Real, measurable, and almost never the reason a stock moves in your lifetime. Not an investment clock.

This is why the scorecard does not stop at whether a company points the right way. A whole measure exists purely to ask about the clock: how fast this particular correction moves, and whether it is visibly moving now, in real orders and approvals and adoption inside the last four quarters. A company aligned with a centuries-long correction and no near-term momentum scores near zero there, by design.

The same caution runs the other way, and it is the honest limit of this whole approach: a business earning from an imbalance on a slow clock can pay you well for decades. Nothing in this framework claims otherwise. What it claims is narrower, and worth stating plainly: when a correction does arrive, it lands on the businesses that were holding the system out of balance, and the ones helping it settle absorb the demand that gets released. Which clock you are betting on is a decision you make, not one the algorithm makes for you.

Step 2 · How it was judged

Turning that idea into something that can actually grade a company

A principle you cannot apply is just an opinion. So the question from Step 1 had to become something checkable. The move that makes it checkable is to stop scoring companies and start scoring what a company does to a stock.

A stock is anything that accumulates and can run out or pile up: water in an aquifer, homes in a city, safe blood in a supply, topsoil on a field, working capacity on a grid, plastic in circulation. Stocks change through flows, the rates going in and out. Balance is not a mood or a virtue. It is a stock whose inflow and outflow match. Every company is a mechanism attached to one of those flows, and that is the thing the engine measures.

That picture is the scorecard. Six measures, 100 points, the same six for every company. The weights are the argument: two thirds of the score is the stock, the flow, and the loop, because those are the only three things that decide whether a business is helping a system settle or holding it open.

20The stock. Name the accumulation it touches. Is it load-bearing, and is it outside its safe range right now?
25The flow. Which direction, and how much correction per dollar of revenue. Needs a measured before and after, not a claim
20The loop. Does succeeding destroy its own demand or manufacture more? And does the revenue survive if the system rebalances?
15Growth pattern. Does it copy a proven unit the way cells divide, or swell? Can it stop growing and stay solvent?
10Buffer. Efficiency that keeps slack, or efficiency bought by removing it. One plant and one customer is fragility, not thrift
10Clock. The Step 1 question, scored. How fast does this correction land, and is it visibly moving in the last four quarters?

Two of those deserve a note, because they are what the earlier version of this engine got wrong.

The loop is the half that gets missed. It is easy to admire a business with a beautiful circular process and never ask what its inputs depend on. A company can run a genuinely closed loop inside a system that has to stay broken for the loop to have anything to feed on. Asking the question directly moved several highly ranked names a long way down, and it is the single biggest change in how this list is built.

Being hard to replace is not automatically a credit. If a company is hard to displace because it does the job better, that shows up as durability. If it is hard to displace because it sits on a chokepoint and charges for the privilege, that is a deduction, because it is holding the stock hostage. The test is whether the company uses its position to restrict the very flow it is supposed to be correcting.

One thing is deliberately not scored. Whether a company can survive long enough to matter is a gate, checked before anything else: under a year of cash with no financing lined up, more than a quarter of the company issued as new shares in three years, a pending buyout, or an auditor doubting it can continue. Fail any of those and it is set aside regardless of score, because a high score on a company that runs out of money is not a smaller success, it is not a result at all. That is a statement about the balance sheet, not about the business.

Then that scorecard was run as a tournament: six rounds, each one harder than the last, every elimination written down with its reason. Scores are not opinions held privately; every survivor and every rejection is recorded and searchable in the next two steps.

The universe is every company an American investor can actually buy: every listing on the NYSE, NASDAQ and NYSE American, plus every SEC-registered filer, plus every foreign company reachable through a US over-the-counter listing or ADR. Wärtsilä of Finland, Coloplast of Denmark and Tomra of Norway all entered that way. It is not every listed company on earth; roughly 60,000 exist across all the world's exchanges, and most trade only in their home market where a US investor cannot reach them without a specialist broker.

A score of 81 means a company earned 81 of those 100 points on the six measures above. The scale is demanding on purpose, and 80 is a high bar rather than a common one. Companies are then grouped into tiers by score, T1 at 80 and above down to T4 at 65, with anything below 65 marked for exit review. The tier is shorthand for how much weight a company might reasonably carry, not a prediction about its share price.

These bands were left exactly where they were when the scorecard was rebuilt, even though the rebuild moved almost every score down. Sliding the bands down to keep the same number of companies in the top tier would have reproduced the old answer by arithmetic and told you nothing. If fewer companies clear the bar, fewer companies clear the bar.

Reference · Full rules

How the grading works, in full

The gate, checked before any scoring

Survivability is not a dimension, because a company that runs out of money did not score badly, it produced no result at all. Any of these sets a company aside regardless of its score: under 12 months of cash with no committed financing · more than 25% of the company issued as new shares over three years · a pending buyout · a going-concern doubt from its auditor. This is a reading of the balance sheet, not a judgment about the business.

The 100-point score, applied to every company the same way

Balance is a property of a stock, meaning something that accumulates and can run out or pile up, and a stock is in balance when its inflow and outflow match. A company is a mechanism attached to one of those flows. The six measures score the mechanism.

  • A · The stock · 20. Name the accumulation in concrete terms. Is it load-bearing, meaning would the system degrade if it left its safe range, and is it outside that range now? No nameable stock caps this at 6. The moat adjustment lives here: defensibility earned by doing the job better is neutral, but defensibility that comes from blocking substitution, a chokepoint on something essential, or single-source control of a load-bearing stock takes −3, because that is holding the stock hostage.
  • B · The flow · 25. Direction and magnitude of the effect on that stock per dollar of revenue. The heaviest single measure. The top band requires a quantified before and after; "appears to" and "likely" score at the bottom of their band, and where no evidence exists the measure is scored low and marked evidence: none.
  • C · The loop · 20. Two questions. Sign (12): does success reduce the demand it feeds on (self-damping) or create more of it (amplifying)? Coupling (8): does the revenue survive if the system rebalances, or does it require the imbalance to persist? A closed loop inside a system that has to stay broken scores low here.
  • D · Growth pattern · 15. Cells divide rather than swell, stop at contact, and dismantle obsolete parts cleanly. Replication (6): copies a proven unit, or grows one entity without limit. Contact inhibition (5): is there a size at which it stays solvent without further growth? Clean exit (4): when the product is obsolete, does it strand assets? An asset that does not go obsolete is not stranded.
  • E · Buffer · 10. Efficiency that keeps slack versus efficiency bought by removing it. Distributed, modular, repairable and multi-sourced scores high. A single global plant, zero-inventory dependency, or one customer above 40% of revenue scores at the bottom. This is the fragility qualifier on B.
  • F · Clock · 10. Time constant (6): months for reimbursement and substitution, years for regulation and technology displacement, decades for infrastructure, centuries for soil and aquifers. Moving now (4): dated orders, approvals, backlog or installed-base growth inside four quarters. Correct alignment with no momentum scores near zero here on purpose.

Automatic disqualifiers

Each of these earns by holding a system out of balance on purpose, which makes the earnings a liability with a delay on it rather than a durable position:
War and weapons · addiction · artificial scarcity (including crypto treasuries) · planned obsolescence · keeping people chronically sick where durable healing is possible · maintaining a broken system that should disappear · concentrated factory-farm waste · profiting from water scarcity rather than reducing it · rapidly inflating system costs · a single uncertain breakthrough · excessive dilution or short runway · stagnant commercialization · undifferentiated commodities.

The clock rule

An imbalance corrects on the clock of whatever does the correcting, which is often far slower than any holding period. Direction without timing is not investable, so measure F carries the timing question directly. A company can be correctly aligned and still be a poor position for thirty years, and a company earning from a slow imbalance can pay well the whole time. The framework's claim is only about what happens when a correction actually lands, not about when it lands. One consequence is worth stating plainly: this scorecard is deliberately hostile to long-clock infrastructure. Every company whose correction runs on a decades scale sits at 3 of 6 on the time constant, and that is measure F working as written rather than a flaw to be patched.

Tiers

  • T1 · Core. Score 80+. Suggested 50–70% of a plan.
  • T2 · Growers. Score 74–79. Suggested 20–35%.
  • T3 · Catalysts. Score 69–73, riskier, often waiting on one specific event. Suggested 5–15%, sized so a total loss wouldn't hurt much.
  • T4 · Weak signal. Score 65–68. The correction is real but slow, partial, or thinly evidenced.
  • Exit review. Below 65. Held only for a stated reason, and the reason is on the record.
  • SAND. Kept by choice outside the rules. 0–10%.

The bands do not move when the scorecard changes. Rebuilding the engine on the stock-and-flow measures moved 42 of 53 companies down and dropped the average by 7 points. Sliding the tier bands down to keep the same number of names in T1 would have reproduced the previous answer by arithmetic. They were left where they were, and T1 got smaller.

Rulings that shaped this list (Aug 27, 2026)

  • Health tags: pb = makes healthcare cheaper or fairer overall · emb = earns through premium prices paid by insurance. Tags get re-checked against evidence when challenged (Tristel confirmed pb at roughly 10× cheaper per use; Cerus moved to emb on evidence of +30% cost per unit).
  • Country risk is scored, not hidden: China-VIE −6 · China-direct −4 · Taiwan-linked −2.
  • Food: distributing industrial food efficiently is optimization, not regeneration. It moves an existing flow faster without changing what the flow does to any stock, so it scores in the neutral band on the flow measure.
  • Two removal rules: a company carrying both a country penalty and a values-sensitive health tag is removed; so is one majority-controlled by an operator of an excluded system.
  • Price is a view, not a merit test. With fractional shares, a $400 stock and a $4 stock are equally buyable.
  • A closed loop is not enough on its own. If the loop's inputs are the waste stream of a system that has to stay out of balance for those inputs to keep arriving, the coupling measure scores it low however elegant the loop is. This is what moved Darling Ingredients down, and the evidence is not hypothetical: management reported the US cattle herd at a 75-year low.
  • Depth: every company on the list is now scored on all six measures with the same evidence standard. The older split between deep and light audits is gone.
